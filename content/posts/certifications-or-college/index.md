Why did I start with CompTIA certifications instead of finishing college first? This post is my answer as a career changer with a family, including what each route gave me and where a degree might still fit later.
{{< /lead >}}

## Where I started

A couple of years ago I was taking college classes two ways: self-paced courses on Study.com, and classes at HACC, the Harrisburg Area Community College. The Study.com IT courses were fine, and I got A grades in all of them. But I understood the same subjects far better the second time round, when they came up again in my CompTIA study.

Funnily enough, the classes I enjoyed most had nothing to do with IT. Mythology and anthropology at HACC were the highlights. Pre-chemistry and biology were the ones I liked least, and I'm glad I took them anyway, because they showed me that science wasn't the direction for me.

## Why I changed course

I started reading about people who had moved into IT and cybersecurity later in life. A lot of them mentioned the **CompTIA trifecta**, meaning A+, Network+ and Security+, and plenty had no college background or degree at all. They were **career changers**, like me.

That route looked far more manageable around work and family. Study.com was self-paced, which helped, but fixed semesters at HACC were much harder to fit around my shifts and home life. Certifications let me set my own pace and still work toward something employers recognise.

Cost mattered too. An exam voucher costs hundreds of dollars. A degree costs thousands.

## What certifications gave me

Starting with **ITF+** gave me a structured path from the foundations upward. It also widened my view of IT. I hadn't realised how many niches and roles there were until I worked through the certification material.

It introduced me to **open source** and **Linux** too. That led me to use WordPress for a client's website, then Hugo for this site and later for theirs, and to run Linux full time on my laptop. My long-term goal is to use Linux in a cloud or infrastructure role.

Networking is the clearest example of what changed. The Study.com course talked about ports, switches and intrusion prevention, but I never saw how they fitted together. That only came from home projects, like opening the right ports on my firewall so my kids' Minecraft server would work, and running services in Podman containers. Building that WordPress website did the same for DNS and IP addresses, because I had to make a real domain name lead to a real site. Once I'd had to make traffic reach a real server, the terms stopped being a list to memorise and became parts of one system I could picture.

That's the biggest difference I felt. My college classes felt like a checklist to get through and an exam to pass. Certification study, plus hands-on projects, feels like learning something I'm going to use.

## How I study now

I use a mix of resources, some free and some paid:

| Resource | How I use it | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| [Professor Messer](https://www.professormesser.com) | Video courses, where I started | Free |
| [BurningIceTech](https://www.youtube.com/@burningicetech) | YouTube explanations, and his Patreon for performance-based question (PBQ) practice | Free, and paid for a while |
| CompTIA official training | A+ and Network+ mock exams, since the course itself was too slow for me | Paid by my employer |
| Sybex books | Network+ reference, which I dip in and out of | Paid |
| [Dion Training](https://www.diontraining.com) | Network+ mock exams, which I'm planning to buy | Paid |
| Claude | Talking through concepts and practice questions, mostly on my phone | $20 a month |

Writing is part of my study method too. For Network+ I wrote a post on every exam domain, which is how the [Network+ series](/series/comptia-network+/) on this site came about. Explaining a topic in my own words shows me quickly whether I really understand it.

Claude is what I reach for when I'm short on time, going back and forth until a concept makes sense.

## What I've seen in job postings

From the postings I've read myself, it's a mixed bag. Lots of entry-level IT support roles ask for a bachelor's degree and years of experience. Others only ask for relevant experience, with certifications like A+ listed as a plus. The better-paying ways in usually ask for more: years of experience, a degree, or both. That's the honest trade-off: a degree opens doors that certifications on their own don't.

My hope is to build enough experience that I never need the degree, so my plan looks like this:

Down the road, I might transfer the credits and certifications I already have into a fully online, self-paced degree. If I'm honest, that would mostly be to tick a box, unless I felt it could teach me something I didn't already know. I think you can learn most of what IT roles need outside traditional schooling. But I enjoy learning either way, so if an employer pays or it becomes affordable, I'll probably end up doing it.

## What I'd say to someone deciding now

If you're young and just leaving school, I'd say take two years for an associate's degree in a computing field, or four for a bachelor's, and you'll be well set up. Do it in a financially responsible way, though. There's no point finishing a bachelor's with a big debt and then starting in a job that doesn't pay much. An associate's, then experience, then transferring into a bachelor's later once you're stable, is a sensible order.

If you're older, have a family, are changing careers, or can't stop working for a long stretch, certifications like CompTIA's are the way I'd go to get a foot in the door. They're easier to fit around your life, you set the pace, and they let you find out whether you even like IT before committing to more. Pair them with solid foundations, a home lab, and a portfolio on GitHub or your own website documenting what you do. That helps make up for the education gap, because it shows you learn and study in your own time.

Even after all that, I haven't landed my first IT role yet. I've had chances to shadow the IT department at work, and I'm hoping an opening comes up. So whatever your situation, keep plodding along, and all shall be well.
